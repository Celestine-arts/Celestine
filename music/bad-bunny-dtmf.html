Plena, hip-hop — Grammy, Album of the Year

DTMF

"I should have taken more photos." A song about the specific ache of realizing a moment mattered only after it's gone.

Bad Bunny Debí Tirar Más Fotos Super Bowl LX halftime

The title translates, plainly, to "I should have taken more photos." Nothing about the song hides behind metaphor.

An album named after its own regret

"DTMF" is the fourth single from Debí Tirar Más Fotos, the Bad Bunny album that would go on to win the Grammy for Album of the Year — a record built around Puerto Rican identity, memory, and the quiet grief of watching people and places change while you weren't paying close enough attention. The song's genre, plena, is a traditional Puerto Rican folk style built for storytelling and communal memory, and Bad Bunny leans into that tradition rather than his more familiar reggaeton register.

Structurally, the song functions like a photo album set to music: specific, plainspoken lines about wishing for more kisses, more hugs, more captured moments with people who might not always be there. It's less a narrative than an accumulation of small regrets, stacked until they add up to something much larger.

a plea to a past version of himself: hold on tighter, notice sooner, don't wait for absence to teach you what matteredthe emotional core of the song's opening verse, paraphrased

A private grief that became a universal one

What turned "DTMF" into more than a personal reflection is how precisely it captures a feeling common to anyone who's left home, or watched home change without them — the particular ache immigrants and expatriates describe when a beloved place keeps moving forward in your absence. Bad Bunny has folded that sentiment into other tracks on the same album, including direct references to Puerto Rican gentrification, but "DTMF" keeps its focus narrow and personal: not a place changing, but a person, a relationship, a version of life you didn't fully appreciate while you were living it.

That specificity is exactly why it traveled so far beyond a Spanish-language audience. The regret at the center of the song doesn't need translation to land.

Why it became the album's emotional center

At Super Bowl LX, Bad Bunny performed "DTMF" alongside his signature party anthems — a deliberate pairing that let the song's quiet nostalgia sit next to his more celebratory material without either one undercutting the other. That contrast is the key to understanding the song's place in his catalog: it's the moment where the party stops, if only for a few minutes, and the reason for celebrating everything else becomes clear — because none of it lasts as long as you assume it will.
