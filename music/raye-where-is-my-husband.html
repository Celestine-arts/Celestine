WHERE IS MY HUSBAND!

A brassy, half-joking demand that turns out to be one of the more sincere songs about modern dating in years.

RAYE This Music May Contain Hope. UK No. 1

She's not pleading for fate to hurry up. She's setting a deadline.

A checklist, sung like a threat

RAYE wrote and produced "WHERE IS MY HUSBAND!" with Mike Sabath, the same collaborator behind her breakout "Escapism," and premiered it as the opener of her Glastonbury 2025 set, backed by a full brass band. The song completed just eleven days before its September release, arrived as the lead single from her second album — and immediately did something unusual for a "looking for love" song: it made searching for commitment sound less like desperation and more like a dare.

Over punchy horns and a throwback big-band arrangement, RAYE lays out exactly what she wants: someone curious, someone who makes her laugh, someone who'll sit through a David Attenborough documentary without complaint. It's playful in its specificity, but the ask underneath — a real, lasting connection — is entirely serious.

The cultural nerve it hit

The song landed in the middle of an online moment that had been building for a while — endless discourse about detachment being fashionable, about "having a boyfriend" being framed as somehow embarrassing, about independence curdling into a kind of performative loneliness. "WHERE IS MY HUSBAND!" pushes back against all of it, loudly and without irony, insisting that wanting real partnership isn't a failure of self-sufficiency.

RAYE has been candid that this isn't a new theme for her — 2023's "Worth It" wanted to be valued, "Oscar Winning Tears" processed heartbreak — but this is her first time being funny and direct about it at the same time. That combination is what made it stick: it's easy to post, easy to dance to, and considerably harder to dismiss than it looks.

An old-fashioned wish, dressed for right now

RAYE has described herself, without embarrassment, as "an old-fashioned girlie" — someone who'd still be thrilled by a stranger buying her a drink and quoting something that moves her. That earnestness is what elevates the song past novelty. It's not mocking the idea of wanting a partner. It's defending it, brass section and all, against a cultural mood that keeps insisting she shouldn't.
