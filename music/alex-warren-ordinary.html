a wedding song before it was ever released as one

Ordinary

Alex Warren borrows the language of worship music to describe falling in love with one person — his own wife, by name, in every quiet way that matters.

Alex Warren You'll Be Alright, Kid 10 weeks, Global No. 1

You're taking me out of the ordinary.

A love letter that happens to rhyme

"Ordinary" is, by Alex Warren's own account, about exactly one person: his wife, Kouvr Annon, whom he met at eighteen and who stayed by his side, sleeping in a car with him, before either of them had anything close to stability. Warren has described their relationship as the kind of rare, uncomplicated love he thinks is genuinely harder to find in a phone-saturated world — and "Ordinary" as his attempt to put language to it worthy of the feeling.

Written with producer Adam Yaron and co-writers Cal Shapiro and Mags Duval — the latter a fellow Christian songwriter — the track leans deliberately on worship-music vocabulary: holy water, angels, a sanctuary. Warren, who is Catholic, has said the biblical references weren't an aesthetic choice so much as the most honest available language for a feeling that size. Some of the song's specific imagery, he's admitted, started as an off-hand joke between friends before it found its way, half-seriously, into the final lyric.

Almost cut, then impossible to cut

Warren has been candid that he didn't like "Ordinary" when it was first written — just an acoustic guitar and vocals, sounding, in his words, like nothing he'd actually release. It wasn't until production added a choir on top of the arrangement that the song clicked for him: what had felt small and unfinished suddenly sounded, in his own description, "insane." That grandiosity is now the song's signature — a chamber-pop ballad that starts hushed and swells into something closer to a congregation singing along.

a note on the reception

Critics have been split — some finding the religious imagery genuinely moving, others calling it overwrought. What's not in dispute is the song's reach: ten consecutive weeks atop the Billboard Global 200, and an eighteen-week (and counting) run leading the Radio Songs chart, among the longest in the chart's history.

Why "ordinary" is the word that carries it

The title does more work than it first appears to. Warren isn't claiming his life became extraordinary because of fame, or money, or any of the usual markers. He's claiming the opposite — that an ordinary life, with an ordinary person, stopped feeling ordinary the moment the right person was in it. That's a small, almost old-fashioned idea to build a stadium-sized ballad around. It's also, probably, why it's ended up soundtracking so many actual weddings: it doesn't promise magic. It promises that the person across from you can make the everyday feel like enough.
