Melodic rap, R&B — Record of the Year

Luther

Two of the most guarded voices in modern music, singing plainly, about a love generous enough to reshape the whole world.

Kendrick Lamar & SZA GNX 13 weeks at No. 1

Built on a foundation of devotion, sung by two artists not typically known for saying things plainly.

Borrowing a legend, on his own terms

"Luther" interpolates Luther Vandross and Cheryl Lynn's 1982 duet "If This World Were Mine," itself a cover of a Marvin Gaye and Tammi Terrell original — a lineage of soul music built entirely around romantic devotion. Clearing the sample came with an unusual condition: the Vandross estate reportedly required that no profanity appear anywhere in the final track, an instruction both Kendrick and SZA honored, giving the song an atmosphere of restraint that stands out sharply against both artists' more confrontational catalogs.

That restraint is the point. Kendrick, an artist who spent much of the prior year in open lyrical warfare with a rival, and SZA, whose biggest songs often sit inside heartbreak and self-doubt, use "Luther" to imagine something neither of their catalogs usually spends much time on: a love stable enough to lift everyone around it, not just the two people inside it.

A love song built to hold two people, not just one

Where the original 1982 duet imagined a private, romantic utopia, "Luther" keeps that same tenderness while widening its scope — the devotion described isn't just personal comfort, it's framed as something that could ripple outward, steadying not just a relationship but everyone standing near it. It's an unusually generous idea for either artist to commit to on record, and it lands specifically because both of them have spent years proving they don't sing anything they don't mean.

Vocally, the two trade lines rather than layering over each other — a conversation more than a performance, each artist meeting the other's register instead of trying to outdo it. That balance mirrors the song's actual subject: two people choosing to move at the same pace, rather than one carrying the other.

Why the biggest song of the year was also the gentlest

In a year dominated by diss tracks, breakup anthems, and songs built around personal grievance, "Luther" won by doing almost the opposite of what was trending — offering something unguarded and hopeful instead. That contrast is likely part of why the Recording Academy singled it out for Record of the Year: not the loudest song of the year, or the angriest, but the one willing to suggest, without irony, that real devotion might actually be enough to make things better.
