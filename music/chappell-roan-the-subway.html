The Subway

A breakup song about the specific ambush of running into someone's ghost on public transit, over and over, for a year.

Chappell Roan Released July 31, 2025 2 Grammy nominations

The relationship ended a while ago. The subway hasn't gotten the message.

A year of hesitation before release

Chappell Roan first debuted "The Subway" at Governors Ball in 2024, dressed as the Statue of Liberty, and spent the following year performing it live at festival after festival while fans begged for a studio version. She's since explained the delay: she spent that year, in her words, trying to figure out exactly how the song should feel — musically, visually, emotionally — before she was willing to let it go.

The wait tracks with the song itself, which is entirely about the difficulty of letting something go on its own timeline rather than yours. Roan has said "The Subway" was written while she was hiding out in Los Angeles from someone she was deeply in love with — not on bad terms, just avoiding the coffee shops and parties they used to share, unable to fully separate from someone who was still, technically, findable.

Grief that arrives by accident

What makes "The Subway" land is how ordinary its triggers are. A stranger's green-dyed hair. Someone wearing the wrong perfume in the wrong room. These aren't grand, cinematic reminders — they're the small, involuntary ambushes that heartbreak actually specializes in, the ones you can't brace for because you didn't see them coming. The devastation isn't the breakup itself. It's how long the ordinary world keeps handing you reminders of it.

The song's most viral line — a promise to move to Saskatchewan if the feeling hasn't faded in four months — started, by Roan's own account, as nothing more than a word that happened to rhyme. That it became one of the most quoted lyrics of the year says something about how much specificity, even accidental specificity, can do for a song's emotional believability.

An ending that isn't really an ending

The song closes not with closure but with a wish for closure — Roan singing that she's still counting down the days until her ex becomes just another face in a crowded train car, indistinguishable from any other stranger. It's an unusually honest way to end a breakup song: not pretending the pain is over, just naming, specifically, what "over" would even look like.
