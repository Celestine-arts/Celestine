◂ CELESTINE / FILM
Joel & Ethan Coen, 2007

No Country for Old Men

A man finds two million dollars in the desert. The desert wants it back.

A thriller that refuses the thriller's usual comfort

No Country for Old Men sets up a familiar chase — a welder finds drug money at a bloody desert standoff and runs, pursued by a hitman with a captive bolt pistol who kills almost arbitrarily, by a coin flip as often as by design — and then declines to give that chase the ending the genre trains you to expect. Llewelyn Moss's death happens off-screen, between scenes, denying the audience the confrontation the whole film has been quietly building toward. It's a deliberate refusal, and it's the point: the film isn't interested in whether the good guy wins. It's interested in whether decency still means anything in a world that has stopped keeping score.

Anton Chigurh, the hitman, is frightening not because he's a mastermind but because he's nearly impossible to reason with or predict — his violence follows a private, near-mechanical logic that has nothing to do with anger or greed. Javier Bardem plays him with an almost total absence of ordinary human affect, which is scarier than any amount of visible menace would have been.

Sheriff Bell spends the whole film one step behind a violence he no longer recognizes, and the film's final scene is just him admitting that, quietly, over breakfast.

Silence as the loudest choice

The Coens shoot most of the film with no musical score at all, letting the wind, the gravel, and the long empty stretches of West Texas highway carry the tension instead. That absence of music is doing enormous work — it makes the handful of violent sequences land with a flat, unglamorous suddenness, and it makes the film's most philosophical passages, mostly delivered by Tommy Lee Jones's aging sheriff, feel less like commentary and more like a man talking simply to fill a silence that unsettles him.

Standing Verdict

Earns it. A crime thriller that uses the genre's own mechanics to argue that the genre's usual comforts — justice, closure, a clean ending — were never guaranteed in the first place.
