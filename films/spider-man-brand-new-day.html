Brand New Day

Everyone forgot Peter Parker. He's the only one still keeping the promise.

DirectorDestin Daniel Cretton
LeadTom Holland
Set4 years after No Way Home
RegisterStreet-level

Being forgotten on purpose

The premise left dangling at the end of No Way Home — a spell that erased Peter Parker from everyone's memory to save the multiverse — turns out to have been worth the wait. Brand New Day picks it up not as a reset button but as a genuine wound: Peter still remembers MJ, Ned, everyone he loves; none of them remember him back. He keeps fighting crime full-time in a city that has no idea who's doing it, and the film is smart enough to treat that isolation as the actual plot, not just a mood board for the opening act.

Director Destin Daniel Cretton, arriving from Shang-Chi, makes the correct and slightly unfashionable choice to shrink the stakes back down to a single city before letting them expand again — no incursions, no timeline math, just a man alone with a job nobody asked him to keep doing.

The film's best trick is refusing to let the loneliness be solved by the plot. Peter gets allies. He does not get his old life back, and by the end, he's stopped visibly expecting to.

The Punisher problem, solved by contrast

Jon Bernthal's Frank Castle and Tom Holland's Peter Parker share a version of the same job with opposite rulebooks, and putting them in the same frame does more character work for both than a dozen lines of dialogue could. Mark Ruffalo's Hulk cameo threatens, briefly, to tip the film back into crossover-event territory it otherwise avoids — it's the one sequence that feels imported from a different, louder movie.

Sadie Sink's villain, kept deliberately obscure through most of the marketing, benefits from that restraint in the theater, though the character's motivations land better as a mood than as a fully worked-out plan — a minor complaint in a film this focused on its lead's interior state.

The best reviews it's had in over a decade

Critical response has been the strongest of Holland's four solo outings, with more than one outlet calling it the best Spider-Man film since Sam Raimi's Spider-Man 2 — a genuinely large claim, and one that mostly holds up on the strength of Holland's performance rather than the plotting around it. The film runs close to two and a half hours, and the pacing shows it in the middle third; the emotional close still lands regardless.

Standing Verdict

Earns it. The best-since-Raimi comparisons are generous but not indefensible — this is the first Holland-era Spider-Man film that feels like it's actually about Peter Parker's character rather than the size of his supporting cast.
