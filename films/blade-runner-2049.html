◂ CELESTINE / FILM
Denis Villeneuve, 2017

Blade Runner 2049

A memory can be manufactured. The ache it produces apparently cannot.

A sequel that slows everything down

Where the original Blade Runner ran just under two hours, 2049 stretches its story past 160 minutes and spends much of that runtime simply sitting in its own atmosphere — Roger Deakins' cinematography turning a flooded, sun-starved Los Angeles into something closer to a held breath than a backdrop. The pacing has drawn real criticism as sluggish, and it's a fair complaint about a film this deliberate; it's also, mostly, the point. K's investigation into a miracle birth among replicants unfolds slowly because the film wants the dread of the question — what does it mean to be born rather than made — to sit with you longer than a conventional thriller would allow.

Ryan Gosling's K is a performance built almost entirely out of withholding: a replicant designed to obey, quietly discovering he might want things beyond his function, and Gosling plays every escalation of that realization as something closer to grief than excitement.

K owns a memory of a childhood wooden horse he was told is real. Whether it actually happened to him turns out to matter less than how completely he needs it to be true.

Joi, and the honesty of an impossible love

K's relationship with Joi, a holographic companion literally designed to please him, could have played as a hollow gimmick. Instead the film treats her affection as genuine and complicated on its own terms, refusing to resolve whether a manufactured feeling is a lesser one — a question the film's ending, involving another hologram offering the same tenderness to a different customer, deliberately declines to answer cleanly.

Where it strains

Jared Leto's Wallace, the industrialist chasing replicant reproduction for commercial reasons, is thinly sketched compared to everyone else in the film, functioning mostly as a philosophical mouthpiece rather than a character. It's the one place the film's patience doesn't fully pay off.

Standing Verdict

Earns it. A rare studio sequel willing to be slower, sadder, and less certain of its own answers than the original it's extending.
