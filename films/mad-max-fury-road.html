FURY ROAD

A two-hour chase scene that somehow finds room for a genuine political argument along the way.

Almost no plot, almost no dialogue, almost no problem

Fury Road strips the road-movie premise down to its studs: drive somewhere, then drive back, with practically every scene in between staged as a moving vehicular battle across the desert. The film trusts its images to carry the story instead of dialogue — most of it was reportedly written with the screenplay as storyboards rather than pages of script — and the result moves with a clarity that far more expensive, more talkative blockbusters rarely manage. You always know where everyone is, who's chasing whom, and why it matters, even at full speed.

What makes the chase mean something beyond spectacle is that it's never actually about Max. Furiosa, smuggling Immortan Joe's captive wives to freedom, is the one making every decision that matters, and Max spends much of the film catching up to a plan she's already three steps into. It's a rare franchise entry willing to let its own title character be the secondary hero of his own movie.

The film's politics never arrive as a speech. They arrive as five women deciding, without asking permission from anyone, that they are more than property — and then driving away.

Practical stunts as a moral choice

George Miller's insistence on real vehicles, real explosions, and real stunt performers rather than digital doubles gives every collision a weight that CGI-heavy action has mostly lost — you can feel the mass of the war rigs, the actual risk in a body thrown between moving trucks. It's not nostalgia for old-fashioned filmmaking. It's a legible physical world, which turns out to be exactly what makes two straight hours of vehicular combat watchable rather than numbing.

Standing Verdict

Earns it, completely. A blockbuster that proved spectacle and substance were never actually in competition — they just needed a director willing to storyboard the whole thing before writing a word.
