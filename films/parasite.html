◂ Celestine / Film
Bong Joon-ho, 2019

Parasite

A house with a basement nobody mentions, and a family that only fits in it by pretending they don't.

the stairs matter more than the plot

The Park house, seen from above

Every room in the Park family's architect-designed home is glass, light, and clean sightlines — a house built by someone who never had to think about what's underneath it. The Kims, hired one by one under false identities, move through this house as staff, learning its rhythms well enough to nearly disappear into them. The film spends its first half enjoying the con as much as the audience does, letting the Kims' cleverness feel like triumph rather than desperation.

Then it complicates that pleasure on purpose. The con works because the Parks are not cruel, exactly — they're just constitutionally unable to see the Kims as anything other than convenient. Kindness from people with total control over your income is its own kind of violence, and the film understands that better than almost anything else made about class in the last decade.

↓ down the stairs, into the flood ↓

The house under the house

The reveal that a second family has been secretly living in the Park house's sub-basement for years, unnoticed, reframes everything the film has shown so far — the Kims were never as far down as they thought they could fall. When the storm floods their own home later the same night, the film cuts between the Parks sleeping through the rain in dry comfort and the Kims wading through sewage to save what little they have, and it does not soften the contrast for a second.

The basement, for both families, is not a metaphor layered on top of the story. It's the actual mechanism of the plot — the thing that gets discovered, fought over, and finally erupts up through the floor at the birthday party that ends the film in violence.

Nobody in Parasite is evil. That's the film's most unbearable idea — decency and cruelty turn out to require the exact same amount of effort, depending only on which floor you live on.
Standing Verdict

Earns it, entirely. A film that treats class not as a backdrop but as literal architecture — floors, stairs, sightlines — and never once has to say the word "inequality" to make the argument land.
