◂ Celestine / Film
Jordan Peele, 2017

Get Out

The politest weekend a boyfriend has ever survived. Barely.

Horror with the volume turned down

For most of its first hour, Get Out withholds anything a horror audience would recognize as a scare — no jump cuts, no score stingers, just a Black man meeting his white girlfriend's parents for the first time and noticing, one small moment at a time, that something about the hospitality is off. A groundskeeper who stares too long and runs at night. A housekeeper who cries without explanation. A party full of older white guests complimenting his physique with an enthusiasm that curdles the longer it goes on. None of it is a scream. All of it is the film building an argument that the horror was never going to arrive with a monster — it was already in the room, disguised as good manners.

The "sunken place," where victims' consciousness is banished while their bodies are used by someone else, is the film's most quoted image, and it works because it's a literalized metaphor rather than a random horror gimmick: being unheard, unable to influence a room you're trapped inside of, dressed up here as body horror instead of everyday experience.

The scariest sentence in the film isn't a threat. It's a compliment, delivered with total sincerity, about how well Chris's body would suit someone else.

A satire that never breaks its own tension

Peele's script is doing satire and horror at the same time without letting either soften the other — the film is genuinely funny in places, largely through Lil Rel Howery's best-friend character, but the comedy never once defuses the dread, because it's aimed at the same target the horror is: a specific, liberal, well-meaning brand of racism that insists on its own innocence right up until the moment it's caught operating a body-snatching auction in the basement.

The one place it simplifies

The film's late-stage explanation of the antagonists' full scheme is more mechanically elaborate than it strictly needs to be, trading some of the earlier atmosphere's ambiguity for exposition. It's a minor cost against a first hour that remains one of the most controlled slow builds in recent horror.

Standing Verdict

Earns it, fully. A horror film willing to trust that the most frightening thing on screen can be a smile that lasts one beat too long.
